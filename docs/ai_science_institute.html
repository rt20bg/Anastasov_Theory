1. Introduction: The Illusion of Completed Science and the Stagnation in Fundamental Physics

One of the greatest paradoxes of modern science is the stark contrast between breakneck technological advancement and the apparent stagnation in our fundamental understanding of the Universe. This is particularly evident in physics, where we have not witnessed a genuine paradigm shift in decades. There is a silent but deep-seated delusion—that in the 19th and early 20th centuries, we discovered almost all the absolute truths, and since then, we have merely been adding details to an already "completed" picture. The odds that we guessed every correct path on the very first try, without needing a major course correction in the last 70 years, are practically zero.

A symptom of this profound problem is the lack of consensus on the most foundational questions. In pop science, concepts are often presented as definitively established facts, but the raw statistics within academic circles paint an entirely different picture. Evidence of this can be found in two massive surveys conducted in 2025–2026 to mark the 100th anniversary of quantum mechanics.

The first, published in the prestigious journal Nature (July 2025), gathered responses from over 1,100 physicists and researchers, with invitations sent to more than 15,000 published scientists in the field. The results were striking: exactly 36% of the respondents cited the orthodox Copenhagen interpretation as their favorite. The remaining votes were scattered across radically different views—17% preferred epistemic approaches, 15% supported the Many-Worlds interpretation, 7% favored pilot-wave theories (De Broglie-Bohm), and so on.

Shortly after, the Big Mysteries Survey (2025–2026), conducted among 1,675 scientists (members of the American Physical Society and readers of Physics Magazine), decisively corroborated this finding: 35.7% (rounded to 36%) once again chose the Copenhagen interpretation.

The significance of these specific numbers is monumental. In earlier years, polls on this topic were primarily conducted during specialized conferences with small sample sizes (between 30 and 150 people), making it easy to dismiss the results. These new data, however, come from a massive sample of actively publishing physicists worldwide. This unequivocally confirms that the lack of a definitive majority on fundamental questions is real and permeates the entire scientific community.

And yet, despite this glaring lack of consensus, the academic system continues to move purely on inertia. The entire financial and institutional might of academia is directed toward research that effectively chooses the safest approach: confirming the status quo and avoiding academic risks that could damage prestige. This prestige is directly tied to the future progression (and grant funding) of academic staff, laboratories, and university departments.

In reality, almost no one today awards subsidies, grants, or even basic salaries to researchers who want to go back to the drawing board and search for meaningful alternative systemic approaches.

But even if funding and brave individuals were found, they immediately collide with a problem insurmountable for humans: the information flood.

Hundreds of thousands of scientific papers are published every year. The vast majority of them are simply information noise—small, safe steps down already well-trodden paths, or purely philosophical musings with no practical value.

It is physically impossible for a single human mind to read, comprehend, and filter this entire ocean of literature to find that one brilliant, alternative idea that was overlooked decades ago.

Science has stagnated not because there is a lack of new ideas, but because the truly valuable innovations are potentially buried beneath a mountain of publications.

At the same time, empirical data for testing new ideas exists today—and in massive quantities. This is especially true within large tech companies, which generate colossal datasets of information.

2. The Concept: AI Institute for Scientific Analysis

Unlike the academic environment, corporate science is rarely concerned with dogmas; it cares about what actually works in practice to achieve engineering advancements. And herein lies the massive opportunity: any unconventional fundamental theory that survives scrutiny and demonstrates real engineering applicability is immensely valuable. The profits and impact of such a discovery would be measured in billions. If we could combine several such breakthroughs—genuine paradigm shifts—they would completely transform entire industries. Whoever is first to discover these alternative paths will inevitably secure an overwhelming advantage.

This is precisely where the need for a completely new approach is born. If human researchers cannot read and sift through the scientific noise to discover the alternatives, we must entrust this task to machines. The solution to this foundational problem is the creation of an entirely new type of scientific entity: an Institute for Machine Scientific Sifting (or simply the AI Institute). This would be a state (or international) institution whose primary goal is to deploy modern artificial intelligence systems to massively and almost instantaneously scan, filter, and evaluate every existing (or future) theory.

Against the backdrop of the enormous potential for technological breakthroughs, the investment in such an endeavor is not just justified—it is mandatory. And here lies a very telling irony. An old university joke tells of a dean complaining to the physicists about why they need such expensive laboratories, whereas mathematicians only need a pencil, paper, and a wastebasket, and philosophers don't even need the wastebasket.

In its initial operational phases, the proposed AI Institute resembles the mathematician from the joke. Its functioning does not require multi-billion-dollar particle accelerators; it only requires compute time, cheap AI tokens, and a few researchers' salaries—an annual overhead comparable to the budget of a third-rate football team from a mid-sized European city. Artificial intelligence will certainly make mistakes during its scanning, but this error rate is not fatal—it is entirely negligible against the colossal scale and low cost of tokens. Only when a concept survives the ruthless sieve of AI analysis and preliminary computer simulations does the time come for precise, expensive physical experiments.

3. Stage 1: The Great Filter

Once the institute is established and armed with the necessary computational resources, its first and most monumental task is to initiate "The Great Filter." This is not a simple keyword search engine, but a complex system of Large Language Models (LLMs) tuned to read, comprehend, and categorize scientific literature on a deep conceptual level.

The process begins with the automated sifting of the giant information noise. The models scan archives containing millions of publications—both historical texts and those just hot off the press—with one primary objective: the ruthless elimination of anything that lacks measurable or testable value!

At this early stage, the algorithms discard obviously weak or factually unsound papers.

Equally important is the removal of purely philosophical interpretations—papers that engage in endless academic debates about the nature of reality, yet offer neither a new mathematical framework nor a predictable physical model.

The theories that survive this initial mass "culling" (a process that often takes mere seconds) advance to the actual machine evaluation. Here, the AI system analyzes them in-depth and scores them across two independent criteria:

  • Scientific Potential: How mathematically robust and logically consistent is the theory? Can it explain existing anomalies more elegantly than the current paradigm?
  • Practical Value: Does the theory hold potential for real-world engineering applications? Does it predict effects that could be harnessed to create new technologies, materials, or energy sources?

The result of this analysis is a matrix from which the institute selects two groups of candidates for further testing. The first group consists of the "straight-A" theories—those with the highest combined scores.

The second, and perhaps more important group, comprises the "high risk, high reward" ideas—highly speculative concepts that might sound crazy and score low on initial plausibility, but possess extreme practical utility if proven true.

This is exactly where the power of cheap AI tokens shines once again. The artificial intelligence will inevitably make mistakes—it will assign high scores to flawed ideas (so-called "false positives"). But this is entirely acceptable. The goal of the Great Filter is not to discover the absolute truth on the first try, but to distill hundreds of thousands of unreadable papers down to a few tens of thousands of concrete candidates that deserve the attention of the second stage: computer validation.

4. Stage 2: Computer Validation (In Silico)

Once the Great Filter has reduced hundreds of thousands of publications down to a few tens of thousands of promising candidates, it is time for the next bottleneck. These theories can no longer be evaluated solely on their text and conceptual logic. They must be put to the test in a computational environment.

This is the stage of computer validation, or in silico testing. For each of the selected theories, modern AI agents generate preliminary simulation models (often based on Python and contemporary physics simulation libraries). Surprisingly, writing, debugging, and running such baseline code often takes barely half an hour and can be executed completely autonomously by the machines themselves.

The goal of these models is to verify whether the theory's mathematical framework actually functions when placed in a dynamic environment. Do the equations break down at extreme values? Do they lead to infinities that lack physical meaning?

But perhaps the most thrilling function of this stage is the "resurrection" of old theories. The history of science is littered with brilliant ideas proposed decades ago that were abandoned or marginalized simply because, at the time, humanity lacked the computational power or the specific empirical data needed to confirm them.

Today, the situation is fundamentally different. The Institute has access to petabytes of modern data—from the open datasets of telescopes and particle accelerators to massive corporate databases. When a forgotten theory from the last century is resurrected and today's datasets are run through its mathematical model, it might suddenly yield results and predictions that perfectly align with reality.

This process is incredibly fast and infinitely cheaper compared to building physical experimental setups. Through aggressive testing with real-world data and computer simulations, this stage successfully separates the ideas that only look good on paper from those that actually work in the digital realm.

At the end of this process, out of the tens of thousands of simulated theories, only the "cream of the crop" remains—a few thousand exceptionally solid or astoundingly promising concepts. Only then do they earn the right to advance to the final, most expensive, and precise phase: testing in physical reality.

5. Stage 3: Precise Experiments and Self-Driving Labs

When the computer simulations conclude, out of the massive initial volume, only a few thousand theories remain that are simultaneously logically sound, mathematically stable, and non-contradictory to existing empirical datasets. It is only at this point that the Institute transitions to true physical-world science.

It is vital to emphasize the exact role of the Institute here: it does not aim to be the ultimate arbiter of truth or a new academic dictator declaring what is right and what is wrong. Its function is much simpler, yet vastly more important—to act as an impartial, objective experimenter.

For each of the surviving theories, a specific, precise physical experiment is designed, with the sole purpose of either confirming or refuting it in the real world.

Naturally, there will be theories that look perfect on paper and in a Python simulation but require energies on the scale of the Solar System to test, or technology that does not yet exist.

These concepts are not discarded as "wrong." Instead, they are cataloged with a clear technical explanation of why they cannot be tested today and are "frozen" for a specific period, say, 5 years. Upon expiration of this period, they are automatically reviewed against humanity's newly developed technological capabilities.

For theories that can indeed be tested with current means, the Institute utilizes or rents the appropriate laboratory infrastructure. But the grand vision for the future of such a project goes much further than standard universities.

The ultimate goal of the Institute is the creation of highly automated facilities, known as "self-driving labs." Imagine robotic setups governed by AI that independently assemble optical tables, mix chemicals, calibrate lasers, or fabricate microchips. These machines can conduct hundreds of experiments around the clock, free from human fatigue or academic bias. This approach would eliminate the greatest bottleneck in modern science—the slow, manual execution of physical experiments. In this way, thousands of promising ideas could be tested in parallel, transforming the Institute into a true, automated factory for paradigm shifts.

6. The Impossible Path of a New Idea: Why Even Einstein Would Fail Today

The contrast between this objective, automated vision for science and today's reality is striking. To understand the true necessity for such an AI institute, we must recognize just how difficult the current mechanism of academic publishing and recognition really is. If Einstein, Planck, or Maxwell returned from the grave today and brought the perfect, completed alternative theory—or even just an interesting alternative—it would be practically impossible for it to get published in a respected journal. Even smaller are the odds that it would ever be read by a renowned contemporary scientist via a randomly sent email.

The path of a new, paradigm-shifting idea today is blocked by an institutional wall. The academic system is built around "peer review"—a process that inherently protects the status quo. Reviewers are experts whose entire careers are built upon the prevailing paradigms.

Any theory that fundamentally contradicts their life's scientific work, no matter how well supported by data, is automatically rejected as "speculative" or "lacking physical meaning," without ever getting a chance for basic consideration. This is not out of malice; it is simply because a journal has extremely limited space and must protect its own academic prestige.

To this social problem, we must add the problem of data interpretation. It is often forgotten that 99.999% of the visible Universe can be explained not only by General Relativity (GR) in its standard form but also by at least a dozen parallel, mathematically equivalent theories developed by eminent scientists. In fact, Einstein himself later developed a second approach—the so-called Teleparallel Gravity—which uses the "torsion" of spacetime instead of its "curvature," yielding perfectly identical predictions for the observable Universe.

The problem is that, right now, all of our modern measuring instruments and data interpretations are calibrated solely and exclusively through the lens of one of these theories. When a new idea tries to break through, it crashes into the fact that the very "evidence" it must compete against has been pre-calibrated to fit the old theory.

Just imagine: a young, promising scientist discovers something peculiar among the stars, which happens to have a slightly better explanation according to one of these parallel theories. They would never have the courage to point it out, because this "slightly better explanation" would be burdened by an entire alternative theory—something absolutely unthinkable for a modern scientist. After all, if they wrote such a dissertation after receiving state grants for years, they would be immediately ridiculed and perhaps even fired! And what manager in a private organization would justify to shareholders that their model works according to an "alternative theory"?! They might even face lawsuits.

Consequently, these small and large discrepancies inevitably accumulate and are ignored, and the data—when it even exists—is simply withheld from the broader public.

It is precisely this institutional and interpretative blockade that renders the human approach completely incapable of breaking out of the stagnation. The human scientist has neither the time (and often, neither the desire) to read thousands of pages of foreign alternatives, nor the career courage to publish something radical. Only an impartial, machine system—devoid of ego and the need for academic approval—can break through this wall.

7. Conclusion: The Economics of the Breakthrough

If we had to summarize the idea of the AI Institute in a single sentence, it is the construction of the most asymmetrical investment vehicle in the history of science.

To comprehend the true scale of this potential, we shouldn't look at traditional heavy industry, but rather at how the high-tech sector operates today. Take, for instance, lithography machines for manufacturing microchips. The entire global artificial intelligence revolution currently hinges on a handful of fundamental discoveries in optics and precision engineering. A single company in Europe (ASML)—by possessing the right discoveries and a validated dataset—holds an absolute monopoly on the machines that physically "draw" modern processors. Without this extremely narrow yet critically important knowledge, the entire AI boom would be completely impossible.

Even more indicative is the example of Nvidia—one of the most important and highly valued corporations in the world today. Despite selling hundreds of billions of dollars' worth of hardware, Nvidia practically owns zero factories that actually manufacture the chips. Their job is to produce pure information. They develop the architecture and send highly complex digital "files" (schematics and instructions) to giant foundries in Taiwan. The Taiwanese factories do only one thing: execute these instructions. Nvidia is essentially transmitting knowledge that is literally "printed" onto silicon.

This is exactly the conceptual operating model of the proposed AI Institute. It will not build enormous metallurgical plants or power stations. Its goal is to produce the most valuable resource: the correct files of knowledge.

Let us illustrate just how crucial it is for machines to take over this role using some real-world figures. Currently, barely 1,900 to 2,000 PhD students in physics graduate in the US each year (according to AIP data for 2023–2024). A large portion of them return to their home countries, and historically, at least half of the remainder pivot to the IT or financial sectors, lured by better prospects and salaries.

Today, there are only about 20,000 officially employed scientific researchers (physicists) in the entire United States. By comparison, the US Postal Service (USPS) alone has over 530,000 employees, and the active US military numbers over 1.3 million. The army of minds tasked with deciphering the Universe is statistically negligible compared to ordinary state administration. After all, what do we prefer: our mail arriving five minutes earlier, or a fundamental technological leap?

We all know how artificial intelligence is literally transforming the IT industry. According to the latest estimates, the demand for junior programmers has crashed by 70% to 80% compared to 2019, simply because a single experienced engineer armed with AI tools can now do the work of an entire team. AI technologies have ALREADY transformed entire industries employing millions of people. Against this backdrop, those 20,000 physicists are scattered across dozens of scientific subfields, frequently traveling the globe to laboratories, or swamped with teaching duties at universities. Practically, they simply do not have the time or ability to track the tens of thousands of new papers. There is simply no human being on the planet capable of such a feat.

The world can no longer wait for some tired professor to open their laptop at three in the morning and stumble upon an interesting alternative theory "to unwind." The odds of them giving it any attention while their inbox is jammed with bureaucratic messages from students, administration, and predatory journals demanding $500 for a "publication" are practically zero (especially since their email is publicly visible on their university website and publications)!

Pitted against this humanly impossible task is the modest investment in an AI Institute. In its initial phases, the overhead is almost negligible—compute capacity, tokens, and a team of engineers costing less than the upkeep of a third-rate football team. Against this stands the potential for unimaginable return on investment (ROI). If the Institute discovers even one correct theory that unlocks a new way to harvest energy or improves an AI chip by just 10%, that knowledge would instantly become a digital blueprint and pay back the investment millions of times over.

But there is something far more important at stake here than money. Right now, the world stands at a geopolitical crossroads. There is a massive economic race underway, and we are on the verge of the opening—and subsequent closing to third parties—of the cosmic door. Such potential new discoveries are not merely something that will excite Wall Street investors.

Let us recall what happened with the discovery of the atomic bomb. In practice, no new country can "freely" build one, even though the theoretical knowledge of the process can be found in any modern textbook.

The future is already knocking at the door. When analogous technologies or weapons are placed in space, the door will inevitably be shut once more, and access to the infinite cosmos will be restricted by a de facto "space council." Who will be first? With what kind of lead? What percentage of the stars—or, much more practically, the Moon or near-Earth asteroids—will be owned by one nation or alliance?

Therefore, such an institute cannot simply wait. Its significance transcends even the boldest national interests. Such an institute must be built, and it must be built today. Its creator, along with everyone involved, will be remembered by history for its discoveries. The royalties from patents will be measured in the billions, and the work will be the most fascinating challenge of the century!

The world cannot afford the luxury of stagnating for decades more, relying solely on the chance that a human mind might perceive the truth amid millions of papers. Whoever is first to entrust the systematization of human knowledge to machines and establishes this Institute will not merely publish the next prestigious paper. They will print the future and hold the key to the door that is very soon destined to close. The question is not whether such an Institute will be created, but who will create it first.
